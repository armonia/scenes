The grammar

Thirty-six movements,
no cuts.

The vocabulary the scenes are built from. A transition is something you pick between two finished clips; a movement carries the film from one state to the next with no join to hide. Every entry here runs a live demo, states the numbers it starts from, and names the bench that can fail it. Twenty-six of them run on the slab; the last ten have no interface on screen at all, because in those the sentence is the film.

Twenty-nine of the thirty-six name a bench that exists, runs in CI and can fail. The other seven are printed in grey: six name a bench nobody has written yet, and one names a bench that exists but cannot run in CI. A measurement nobody runs is a proposal, and this page would be doing the thing it warns against if it let the two look alike.

Forty-five seconds, one shot

This is the film the vocabulary is for. Twelve of the thirteen beats render and join end to end. The one still in grey is the truck, and it is not grey for lack of time: any clipping — overflow: hidden, overflow: clip, clip-path — flattens preserve-3d, and the slab has to clip, so per-layer parallax costs a restructure of the DOM in every scene rather than a few translateZ. It stays printed the way the missing benches are printed: a plan and a thing are not allowed to look alike.

What renders today is six scenes joined end to end, every join measured by seam.sh: UIMockup, CardHandoff, CardFocus, CardRelease, PromptInput, BoardOrbit. 1460 frames, 48.7 seconds, no cut anywhere in them, and the five joins read 557, 64, 55, 29 and 15 pixels. They get cleaner as they go, which is what happens when both sides take the same geometry from the same module instead of agreeing by hand. CardRelease is not a beat in this list because this list is the plan and not the inventory.

Speed is a number

Motion design is dense: the references behind this vocabulary run 13 to 42 seconds and fit more into them than there is in these 48. The tempo of a scene is now its duration — each scene writes its beats against a reference length and durationInFrames scales all of them, so halving it halves every beat inside rather than cutting the tail off. What does not scale is the perceptual thresholds: the four frames between a click and its consequence are the window in which the eye ties a gesture to its effect, not a rhythm decision, and at double speed they would be two. Same for the three frames the card lags behind the hand, which are the weight of the object, and for the caret's blink, which is a frequency. Scaling those along with the rest is how a faster scene becomes a broken one.

The negative control gets written first. A bench that cannot fail measures nothing, so it is made to exit non-zero on a broken input before its green is worth anything.
